Structural Inelasticity, Capacity Firming, and Systemic Resilience: Analyzing the 2026 Energy Shock

What wind, solar and batteries can replace during a fuel shock, and what an operator must prove before retiring a gas turbine.

Neo T. Maredi Published 9 November 202613 min read

IN THIS ESSAY

1. The shock and who paid

On 12 March 2026, the International Energy Agency reported that tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz had nearly stopped. [1] [6] The European Brent spot price averaged $103.13 a barrel in March, up from $70.89 in February. [5]

In Lebanon, private generator subscribers paid for diesel through their electricity tariff. The ministry’s base rate for metered consumption rose from 30,244 Lebanese pounds per kWh in February to 40,580 in March, about 34%. The fixed monthly charge stayed the same. How much a household paid depended on how many kWh it bought. [2] [3]

Saudi buyers faced a different pricing question. On 1 January, before the disruption, Riyadh Cement reported that revised fuel prices from Saudi Aramco would raise its production costs by an estimated 6%. The notice puts a date and a figure on the company’s exposure. Its fuel bill was already set to rise when shipping through Hormuz slowed. [8]

Once a price rise reaches a site, how quickly can its equipment switch to another energy input? Atkeson and Kehoe show how equipment choices can constrain the short-run response to an energy price rise. [11] A site with a live grid connection sized for its load can move work off a diesel generator. A site waiting for that connection has to keep buying fuel, cut output or stop. Every day it takes to switch adds another day’s fuel purchases at the new price if the site keeps running.

A site can ride out a missed fuel delivery if it can switch before its tank empties. A working grid connection, a wind farm already exporting power and a charged battery can help during that interval. A project awaiting connection can only change the site’s exposure to a future shock.

From a diesel bill to a grid decision

Consider a facility with electric pumps and a diesel generator. If the grid can carry the pumps, electricity generated from wind or solar can displace diesel at the site. A kiln with a fuel-fired burner faces another constraint. Changing the electricity supply does little for its process heat until the equipment can use it. The same oil price reaches two businesses with different ways out.

At grid scale, the fuel saved depends on which generator reduces output. Displacing an oil-fired unit cuts oil use. Displacing a gas turbine saves gas at the price its operator pays. Brent provides the context for the shock. The plant’s fuel contract and dispatch record provide the evidence for its savings.

The harder decision is whether the grid can give up a turbine after it starts saving fuel. Wind changes when the system needs stored energy. A battery changes how long the operator can wait for the next supply. The following retirement test puts a number on both effects.

2. Three tests for replacing a gas turbine

How much electricity can the portfolio produce in a year?

Take a 500 MW simple-cycle gas turbine operating at an assumed 85% annual capacity factor. It generates 3,723 GWh in a year. Compare solar alone with a mix of solar and wind. The table assumes 25% for solar and 35% for wind, all on an AC basis. These are illustrative inputs for an 8,760-hour year. For a real unit, the starting point is its metered output and dispatch hours.

The mixed portfolio matches the annual total with 200 MW less nameplate capacity than the solar-only option. Both totals precede curtailment and storage losses. To count fuel saved, line up the hourly output with the fuelled generation the operator can turn down. A surplus on a mild afternoon cannot displace a turbine that was already idle.

Can it serve the load when the grid is short?

National Grid SA recorded its 2024 peak of 74,771 MW at 15:00 on 20 August. Solar could contribute then. By 21:00 the panels have stopped generating, while a wind farm can still export and a battery can still discharge. The most difficult hour depends on how much demand remains after available generation and imports. [7]

Dumat Al Jandal gives the wind question a Saudi scale. GASTAT records 400 MW of commissioned capacity, but an operator deciding whether to retire a turbine needs Dumat’s output in the hours the grid was short. Coastal measurements show why location matters. Rehman and Ahmad analysed 1970-1983 hourly records. At 10 metres, mean wind speeds around 4.5 m/s or higher extended to about 18:00 at Dhahran and 21:00 at Yanbu. These are historical patterns at the weather stations. Translating them into MW at a modern wind farm requires hub-height data, a turbine power curve and actual availability. [17] [18]

An evening sea breeze can preserve stored energy for later in the night. A lull across several connected wind sites can draw those batteries down together. Capacity credit measures how much a resource contributes to meeting a reliability target. Estimating it requires wind and demand records with matching timestamps, including the difficult years. [12] [22]

One evening with the same amount of wind

Saudi Energy reports that it commissioned 8 GWh of battery storage across four sites in 2025. One of them, Bisha, is rated at 500 MW and 2,000 MWh. Dividing energy by power gives four hours at the rated output from a full usable charge. That rating alone tells a dispatcher little about what remains at 21:00. [19] [20]

For a smaller worked example, suppose the rest of the fleet leaves 100 MW to cover from 21:00 to 01:00. Solar output is zero. The battery can discharge 100 MW and deliver 400 MWh when full. It starts half full, with 200 MWh deliverable at the grid connection. In each column below, the wind farm produces 200 MWh over four hours. Only its timing changes.

Hour startingSteady wind (MW)Late wind (MW)Battery and load with late wind

With steady wind, the battery supplies 50 MW each hour and carries the site to 01:00. With late wind, the battery is empty by 23:00. In that hour another source must deliver 50 MWh or that much demand goes unserved. By midnight there is surplus wind to charge the battery. The earlier hour has already passed.

If the wind stays still for a second evening, the charging opportunity between the two evenings matters. At 85% round-trip efficiency, the planning assumption in NREL’s 2024 benchmark, an empty battery needs about 471 MWh of charging electricity to deliver 400 MWh later. A gas unit used to recharge it will burn fuel. Charging from wind or solar that would otherwise be curtailed has a different fuel cost. [21]

What happens to frequency if another generator trips?

A synchronous turbine’s rotor slows the initial fall in frequency. Some wind turbines can briefly draw on energy in their rotating blades, then need to regain speed. A PV plant can raise output in daylight if it has held some back. A battery can respond quickly in either period if the inverter has spare MW and the cells have charge. A 100 MW battery already exporting 100 MW cannot simultaneously offer another 20 MW of upward response. [13]

Before retirement, the operator would check how fast and how far frequency falls after a credible trip, then identify the unit that sustains the replacement power after the initial response. This is a separate test from getting through four hours of demand. [13]

3. What substitution costs the system

Two Saudi awards put prices on the comparison. In the July 2025 procurement round, the announced levelised cost was $12.90/MWh for the 3,000 MW Bisha solar project and $20.57/MWh for the 2,000 MW Starah wind project. Those are generation figures at award. The Bisha solar award and the Bisha grid battery are separate projects. [4] [9] [19]

What is a MWh delivered after sunset worth?

Suppose the buyer could charge at the Bisha solar figure and the battery returned 85% of the input. The charging electricity alone would cost $15.18 for each MWh delivered. Against $20.57 for a MWh of wind arriving in that same hour, the difference is about $5.40/MWh for storage equipment, operation and other delivery costs before the two options reach parity. This calculation assumes wind is available at the load and solar energy can be bought at that price. Neither contract specifies this hypothetical delivery service. [9] [21]

Either option can be cheaper in practice. Surplus solar with a low charging price favours storage. A run of windy evenings can reduce battery cycling and the energy capacity the buyer needs. If the wind line is congested or the battery needs replacing sooner than planned, the delivered price changes. Compare the cost of two portfolios serving the same load through the same weather and outage years.

California’s September 2022 heat wave shows how dispatch incentives can affect the evening reserve. Batteries supplied 2.4% of generation in the California ISO area between 16:00 and 21:00 over the ten-day event. On 6 September, high prices earlier in the day drew energy out of storage sooner than usual. A buyer paying for evening availability has a reason to specify how much charge the operator must preserve and when. [23]

Which costs belong in the comparison?

Ueckerdt and colleagues define System LCOE per additional MWh of variable renewable generation at a specified level of deployment. Their framework separates the cost effects of when output arrives, where it enters the grid, and deviations from forecast. [14]

System LCOE = marginal generation cost + marginal integration cost

Generation and timing. Start with plant costs. Trace any curtailed output and the cost of keeping backup units ready for fewer running hours. For storage, include charging, losses, capital, maintenance and the extra cells needed to sustain contracted capacity as the battery ages. Count the backup or storage expense once. [14] [21]

Grid and balancing. Check whether the wind farm can export during the evening deficit and whether the battery is on the side of the constraint where power is needed. Schedule sunset in advance. Price the operating reserve needed if the wind forecast is wrong or equipment trips. [12] [14]

A Saudi-wide cost estimate would require hourly dispatch and network constraints, delivered fuel prices, asset costs and contracts. The two project prices tell a buyer where to start that calculation.

4. Substitution and resilience

A missed diesel delivery and a delayed replacement inverter affect a site on different schedules. Diesel stocks set how long the generator can run. A functioning solar or wind plant can keep producing while a parts shipment is delayed. Once a converter fails, a compatible spare and the time needed to install it can decide how long the affected capacity stays offline. Solar and battery manufacturing are concentrated in a small number of locations. Operators can examine their spares and repair lead times alongside their fuel stocks. [10]

The evidence for retiring the turbine

Run the existing fleet and the proposed replacement against the same hourly demand and weather years. Keep hot afternoons, low-wind nights and consecutive cloudy days in their original order. Carry the battery’s charge forward hour by hour. Include transmission limits, thermal outages, fuel availability and the battery’s usable capacity at the end of its contract. Resetting storage to full every evening would hide the very risk the study is meant to measure. [12] [21] [22]

For each portfolio, report fuel burned by type, total cost, expected hours of shortage and expected MWh unserved. Fuel savings support running the gas unit less. Retirement requires the replacement portfolio to meet the operator’s reliability criterion after the unit is removed and to pass the separate frequency test. If keeping the turbine for a few difficult hours meets the criterion at lower cost, retaining it is a defensible outcome.

Acwa (formerly ACWA Power) can show a developer’s measured output and battery warranties. National Grid SA can test whether that power reaches the load through a credible outage. Saudi Energy Company (formerly Saudi Electricity Company) can bring operating and storage records to that comparison. The Saudi Power Procurement Company, as Principal Buyer, can specify the delivery hours, the reserve obligation and the availability the bids must cover. [7] [9] [15] [16] [20]

Return to 23:00. Two portfolios have produced the same wind energy by the end of the night. One kept the load supplied. The other needed 50 MWh from elsewhere before midnight. An operator who can answer the 23:00 question can decide how much fuel to keep on site, how much charge to hold back and whether the turbine can retire.

Checking the figures

Annual generation. 500 × 0.85 × 8,760 = 3,723,000 MWh. The wind and solar case gives (500 × 0.35 + 1,000 × 0.25) × 8,760 = 3,723,000 MWh before losses.

Costs and losses. Bisha solar $12.8989 ÷ 0.85 = $15.1752/MWh for charging energy. Starah wind $20.5712 − $15.1752 = $5.3960/MWh. The 85% is a benchmark assumption. [9] [21]

Metered generator rate. (40,580 ÷ 30,244 − 1) × 100 = 34.18% for the energy charge. [2] [3]
